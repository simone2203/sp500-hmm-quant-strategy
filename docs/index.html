Market Regime Detection Using Hidden Markov Models (HMM) on SPY

Explore the results Notebook Repository

A two-state Gaussian hidden Markov model separates calm and volatile phases of the US equity market. A walk-forward backtest then tests whether those regimes still help a long/cash strategy when the model can only use information available at the time.

Abstract

The model is a two-state Gaussian HMM fitted on SPY’s daily log returns and 20-day volatility. Traded as a long/cash rule, with monthly refits on past data only, execution one day after each signal and a 0.10% cost per trade, it reduced annualized volatility from 16.6% to 9.8% and maximum drawdown from −24.5% to −14.7% relative to Buy & Hold between October 2020 and September 2026. It also earned about half the annual return, and its Sharpe ratio stayed below Buy & Hold’s. The in-sample backtest’s Sharpe ratio of 1.11 did not survive the removal of look-ahead bias.

The research question

Equity markets do not behave the same way all the time. Long stretches of steady gains with small daily moves alternate with shorter, turbulent phases in which volatility jumps and returns become erratic. An investor who cares about risk would like to know which phase the market is in, but the phase itself is never observed: only prices are.

A hidden Markov model formalizes exactly this situation. It assumes an unobserved state that follows a Markov chain, so that tomorrow’s regime depends only on today’s, and lets each state generate the observed data from its own probability distribution. Fitting the model estimates those distributions and how persistent each regime is; decoding then assigns the most likely regime to every day.

Why “hidden”

The regime is a latent variable. The model never sees a label such as “crisis” or “calm”; it infers two groups of days whose returns and volatility look statistically alike, and it is up to the analyst to interpret them.

Can regimes identified by a two-state Gaussian HMM support a long/cash strategy on SPY that reduces risk relative to Buy & Hold, once the model is restricted to the information available at the time of each decision?

Approach

  1. Data. Daily adjusted closes of SPY from Yahoo Finance, September 2017 to September 2026.
  2. Features. Daily log return and 20-day rolling volatility, standardized before fitting.
  3. Model. A two-state Gaussian HMM with full covariance, fitted by expectation–maximization with hmmlearn.
  4. Rule. 100% SPY in the calm regime, 100% cash in the volatile one. No leverage, no short positions.
  5. Validation. An in-sample backtest first, then a walk-forward backtest with monthly refits, two execution assumptions and transaction costs.
2

Data, features and the model

SPY, the exchange-traded fund that tracks the S&P 500, offers a long and liquid history and reflects the broad market rather than a single stock. The notebook uses its adjusted close, which incorporates dividends and splits, so that the regimes reflect market behavior rather than technical artifacts. The download window is fixed, nine years ending on 22 September 2026, to keep the analysis reproducible: 2,260 trading days, with no missing values.

Each day t is described by two features. The log return compares consecutive closes and is additive over time; the 20-day rolling volatility is the standard deviation of the last 20 log returns. The first 20 days have no complete window and are dropped, which leaves 2,240 observations, from 23 October 2017 to 22 September 2026.

rt = ln(Pt / Pt−1)

σt = sd(rt−19, …, rt)

Both features are standardized (z-scores) before fitting. Their raw variances are of the order of 10−4 and 10−5, small enough for hmmlearn’s default covariance regularization to distort the estimates. The in-sample model uses full-sample means and standard deviations; each walk-forward refit uses those of its own training window.

A two-state Gaussian hidden Markov model

The model has two hidden states. In each state the standardized feature vector xt follows a bivariate normal distribution with its own mean and a full covariance matrix, so the model can also capture how the two features move together. The parameters are estimated with the expectation–maximization algorithm (hmmlearn’s GaussianHMM, fixed random seed), and the most likely sequence of states is decoded with the Viterbi algorithm.

The number of states is fixed at two for simplicity, not because two was shown to be optimal. Fitted once on the whole history, EM converged after 16 iterations, with a final log-likelihood of −4,279.63.

P(St = j | St−1 = i) = aij

xt | St = k ~ N(μk, Σk)

Two states, each with a mean vector μk and a 2×2 covariance matrix Σk, plus the transition probabilities aij.

What the in-sample model found

The labels 0 and 1 that the algorithm assigns are arbitrary, so each state is read from the days assigned to it. The calm regime is defined as the state with the higher mean return, and the notebook then checks that it also has the lower mean volatility: here the two criteria agree. Calm days are more frequent, rise more on average and swing far less.

In-sample means hindsight

Because this model is fitted on the full history, the regime it assigns to any day was estimated with knowledge of what came after. The upper band of Figure 1 is a retrospective description of the market, not a signal that could have been traded. Section 3 removes that advantage.

3

From regimes to a strategy, without look-ahead

The trading rule

The rule is deliberately simple: hold SPY with all the capital when the current regime is calm, and hold cash otherwise. There is no leverage and no shorting, and cash earns nothing. Because the labels can swap from one fit to the next, the rule never refers to “state 0”; it refers to whichever state is calm according to the data the model was trained on.

A first, biased backtest

The preliminary backtest applies the rule to the in-sample states with a one-day lag, so that a position decided at the close of day t earns the return of day t+1, and without transaction costs. The lag avoids using a day’s signal to earn that same day’s return, but it cannot fix the deeper problem: the states themselves were estimated with the whole history.

Even with that advantage, the strategy earned less than Buy & Hold over the full sample (CAGR 11.5% against 15.0%), but with much smaller swings: a Sharpe ratio of 1.11 against 0.74 and a maximum drawdown of −10.4% against −33.7%. The notebook treats these figures as optimistic, not as realistic performance.

wt = 1 if St = calm, else 0

In-sample strategy return on day t: rt · wt−1, with no costs. The strategy is invested on 68.2% of the days.

Walk-forward design

The walk-forward backtest repeats the analysis the way it could have been run in real time: at every point, the model only knows the past.

  1. Warm-up. The first 756 trading days, about three years up to 22 Oct 2020, are used only for training. With less data, each state’s mean vector and 2×2 covariance matrix, and the transition probabilities, would be estimated with high variance. The strategy holds cash throughout.
  2. Monthly refits. The model is refitted every 21 trading days on an expanding window of all the observations before the refit date: 71 refits, from 23 Oct 2020 to 2 Sep 2026. Daily refits would be computationally heavy and would add noise; yearly refits would leave the model on stale parameters.
  3. No leakage in preprocessing. Each refit standardizes the features with the mean and standard deviation of its own training window.
  4. Dynamic relabeling. At each refit, the calm state is identified again on the training data, as the state with the higher mean return, and checked to have the lower mean volatility.
  5. Causal decoding. For each day t until the next refit, the regime is the last element of the Viterbi path computed on all observations up to t. Decoding a whole month at once would let later days revise the earlier ones.
Parameters

Warm-up 756 days. Refit every 21 days. Up to 200 EM iterations per refit. Cost 0.10% per buy or sell.

EM used between 13 and 39 iterations per refit (median 24). No refit reached the limit, and none stopped on a decreasing log-likelihood.

Label switching is not a theoretical concern here. The raw label of the calm state changed 16 times across the 71 refits. With a hard-coded “state 0”, the strategy would have silently invested in the volatile regime after each switch, with no visible error. With dynamic relabeling, the state chosen as calm had both the higher mean return and the lower volatility at every refit.

Execution timing and costs

Signals are computed from closing prices, so a decision taken with the close of day t cannot be traded before that close is known. The notebook brackets execution with two assumptions:

  • shift(2), the main backtest. The order is executed at the close of day t+1, and the first return earned with the new position is that of day t+2.
  • shift(1), a sensitivity check. The order is executed at the same close of day t that produced the signal. This is optimistic, because it requires trading at a price that is only known at that moment.

Both versions pay 0.10% of capital on every buy or sell, charged on the day the executed position changes. Over the walk-forward period, each version changed position 29 times.

ln(1 − c · |Δwt|), c = 0.001

Costs enter as log returns on the execution day, so they compound with the rest of the series.

Results

The four series are compared over the same window, from the first day the walk-forward strategy could hold a position to the end of the data: 26 October 2020 to 22 September 2026, 1,483 trading days, about 5.9 years. Every series is rebased to 1.0 at the close of 23 October 2020, the end of the warm-up, so the comparison is not distorted by three years in which the walk-forward strategy could not invest.

Definitions

CAGR uses 252 trading days per year. The Sharpe ratio is the annualized mean of the daily log returns divided by their annualized standard deviation, with a zero risk-free rate. Maximum drawdown is measured from the starting capital of 1.0.

What the results show

Finding 1

Less risk, not a better risk-adjusted return

Both walk-forward versions cut annualized volatility from 16.6% to about 9.8%, and maximum drawdown from −24.5% to −14.7% with shift(2) or −12.1% with shift(1). They also roughly halved the CAGR, to 8.5–9.1% against 16.2%, and their Sharpe ratios, 0.83 and 0.89, did not exceed Buy & Hold’s 0.90. Over this period the strategy reduced risk without improving the return per unit of risk.

Finding 2

One day of execution delay matters

Moving from shift(1) to shift(2) costs about 0.6 percentage points of CAGR and 0.06 of Sharpe ratio, and deepens the maximum drawdown from −12.1% to −14.7%. The delay weighs mostly on risk, but it does not change the conclusion: under neither assumption does the strategy beat Buy & Hold on Sharpe ratio.

Finding 3

Look-ahead bias flatters the in-sample result

The in-sample strategy and walk-forward shift(1) share the same execution timing. Going from one to the other lowers the CAGR from 12.3% to 9.1% and the Sharpe ratio from 1.11 to 0.89, a gap due to look-ahead bias and transaction costs. The in-sample Sharpe of 1.11 is the most flattering number in the notebook, and it is exactly the one that does not survive out of sample.

The main result is not a strategy that beats Buy & Hold. It is an honest measure of the trade-off between return and risk, obtained with a backtest that removes look-ahead bias and accounts for execution and costs.

Limitations

  • One instrument, one periodSPY from 2017 to 2026: a market dominated by a strong bull run, with the 2022 bear phase. The results may not carry over to other assets or other periods.
  • Model assumptionsTwo states fixed in advance, Gaussian emissions and a first-order Markov chain. The 20-day volatility is serially dependent by construction, so the assumption that observations are independent given the regime holds only approximately.
  • Simplified executionA fixed cost of 0.10% per trade, with no slippage and no modeling of bid–ask spreads or market impact.
  • Idle cash and the risk-free rateCash earns nothing, although for much of the period it would have yielded a significant return, which penalizes the strategy. The Sharpe ratio also assumes a zero risk-free rate.
  • Overfitting and model riskThe walk-forward design removes the look-ahead bias in estimating and decoding the regimes. It does not remove the risk of overfitting, model uncertainty, or the differences between a simulation and live trading.
  • No guaranteeThese are historical simulations under simplifying assumptions. They are not indicative of future returns.
